### 1. [What Does AI-Native Mean for a Product Org?](https://runthebusiness.substack.com/p/what-does-ai-native-mean-for-a-product)
*Run the Business* — Ibrahim Bashir — Nov 17, 2026  `#Org Design`  `#AI Strategy`

Ibrahim Bashir argues that when executives ask a product leader to make the org 'AI-native,' the phrase can mean the product surface, the operating model, or the business strategy. Each meaning implies different investments and owners. He recommends first clarifying which one is being asked for, then tailoring the response to the company's specifics.

**Why it matters**: Directly addresses how a VP should respond when execs ask for an AI-native org, clarifying scope before acting

- **Ask your CEO which AI-native meaning they intend.** Clarify whether they mean the product surface (AI-infused features), the operating model (AI-driven SDLC rituals), or business strategy (how you choose bets), and get a specific definition and timeline.
- **Audit your current features for AI integration.** List where AI already wraps or powers your product, identify the highest-value user workflows where it could add real value, and prioritize those.
- **Map your rituals and ceremonies to an AI-enabled development lifecycle.** Review sprint planning, reviews, and roadmapping to find where AI tooling can speed up or change decisions, and pilot one change with a single team.
- **Reframe strategy around how you win, not just what you ship.** Evaluate your bets by asking which advantages AI makes more or less defensible, and shift roadmap discussions toward competitive positioning rather than feature output.
- **Tailor the transformation to your company's situation.** Assess your growth stage, retention risks, and team readiness before committing to a plan, since the right approach depends on your unique circumstances.

### 2. [The B2B buyer journey with AI](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-b2b-buyer-journey-with-ai)
*Growth Unhinged* — Kyle Poyar — Nov 18, 2026  `#Positioning`  `#AI Strategy`

Kyle Poyar argues B2B buyers use AI engines mostly in the middle of the funnel to compare specific vendors. Brands therefore need to shape what AI says about pricing, functional fit, and compatibility. He also argues that a brand's position on the AI shortlist matters as much as being mentioned at all. These points suggest product marketing and product teams should own accurate, structured product facts.

**Why it matters**: Shows how AI engines shape enterprise vendor comparison, so pricing and fit messaging matter for positioning

- **Write AI-searchable pages for mid-funnel evaluation.** Create specific pages for each persona, industry, and use case, and use real questions from sales calls as keyword research, since they often have no volume in traditional search tools.
- **Make your pricing easy for AI to read.** Opaque pricing is a risk from the first prompt, especially for AI products, where about 10% of top-of-funnel queries for coding agents and website builders asked about cost.
- **Describe your functional fit and tool compatibility clearly.** Buyers ask AI about what your product does and how it integrates before any demo, so audit and update content to explain these consistently everywhere.
- **Track your rank on the AI shortlist, not just mention rate.** Being fourth in an AI recommendation is like landing on page two of Google, so monitor where you're placed in 'best X' and 'recommend X' answers.
- **Audit what AI says about you, not just whether it cites you.** Check the content of AI answers for pricing, fit, and compatibility claims, and fix outdated or missing information that could get you dropped from the shortlist.

### 3. [5 Ways You’re Misusing ICE Scoring](https://itamargilad.com/ice-antipatterns/)
*Itamar Gilad* — Itamar Gilad — Nov 17, 2026  `#Roadmapping`  `#Metrics`

Itamar Gilad argues ICE scoring is useful for prioritization discussions but is often misused. Common errors include overconfident scores, treating the score as a planning output, and adjusting metrics to favor preferred ideas. He argues it works best as an iterative, evidence-driven way to choose which ideas to validate or deliver.

**Why it matters**: Practical guidance for roadmap prioritization hygiene in a growth-stage org

- **Keep confidence low until evidence exists.** Start new ideas at around 0.1 out of 10 and raise confidence only as tests and experiments validate them, not because of opinions, consensus, or a polished pitch deck.
- **Don't rank by the ICE score alone.** Use the score only to choose between ideas with similar confidence levels, and compare impact, ease, and confidence separately when that gives a clearer picture.
- **Use ICE for evaluation, not for locking in a roadmap.** Re-score ideas as validation progresses, and plan around outcomes rather than a fixed list of outputs.
- **Fix the metric before scoring and resist moving the goalposts.** Decide upfront which metric the idea should affect, and treat added justifications like 'it helps future features' as a separate decision rather than a way to inflate impact.
- **Re-evaluate ideas across the full discovery-to-delivery lifecycle.** Run ICE at each validation step, and move an idea into delivery only once it has been sufficiently validated.

**4.** [Your customers don’t just want an agent. They want agents.](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/ai-agent-messaging-study) — *Growth Unhinged* · Nov 16, 2026  `#AI Strategy`  `#Positioning`

Simply calling an AI product an "agent" did not make software buyers more willing to pay, and may slightly reduce interest. Framing the same product as a "team of agents," each handling a distinct function, significantly increased purchase intent, possibly due to the unpacking effect.

- **Don't rely on the 'agent' label alone:** Describing a product as a single agent did not increase buyer willingness to pay and slightly lowered interest (p = 0.084, not significant), so the label isn't a reliable conversion lever.
- **Describe your product as a team of specialized agents:** The multiple-agent framing raised purchase intent by 6.3 percentage points over the single-agent version (p = 0.022). Break your capabilities into named functions, such as a research agent or scheduling agent.
- **Change only the messaging when testing positioning:** The study kept features and page design identical and varied only the header, subheader, and feature labels. Use the same discipline in your own tests so you can attribute results to the wording.
- **Measure willingness to pay, not just perceived quality:** Framing shifted purchase intent without changing ratings of helpfulness, ease of use, or trustworthiness. Include price and purchase questions in your messaging research.
- **Test whether unpacking your features drives interest:** The authors suggest the unpacking effect explains the team framing's lift, since splitting one capability into several named roles may make the offering feel more substantial. Test this with your own feature breakdowns.

**13.** [TBM 452: Contexting vs. Context](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-452-contexting-vs-context) — *The Beautiful Mess* · Nov 18, 2026  `#AI Tools`  `#Org Design`

Highly experienced people struggle to make their tacit knowledge explicit, and context-sharing breaks down on both sides of the exchange. The author argues that instead of forcing humans and AI to rely on explicit context alone, we should design AI to take part in the way people develop shared context through working together.

- **Ask for context by observing the work, not just the explanation.** Pair on a recent decision, watch someone handle a real problem, or review an actual artifact instead of asking them to describe their reasoning in the abstract.

- **Build shared context through the work itself rather than over-engineering AI inputs.** When you're tempted to build elaborate context systems for AI, consider whether a meeting or joint working session would create the shared understanding faster.
